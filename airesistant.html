Defining AI-Resilience

An **AI-resistant assignment** (or AI-resilient assignment) is designed to require a level of **personal context, critical application, synthesis of unique course content, or hands-on skill execution** that current generative AI tools cannot authentically replicate without significant, detectable human intervention.

The goal is to shift the assessment from *what* students produce to *how* and *why* they produced it, emphasizing higher-order thinking skills crucial for fields like Cybersecurity and AI.

1. Process-Based & Scaffolded Assessments 🏗️

Method: Scaffolding and Process Documentation

This tactic shifts the focus from a single, high-stakes final product to the **learning journey**. By breaking down a large assignment into sequential, graded steps, you force students to demonstrate original effort at critical points.

Example: Cybersecurity Risk Analysis

Instead of one final report, require:

  1. **Topic Proposal & Custom Sources:** Identify a unique, local security target and submit an annotated bibliography including two proprietary/lecture-specific readings.
  2. **Drafts/Outlines:** Submit a detailed outline of the methodology and initial findings.
  3. **Final Process Memo:** A short reflection detailing the thought process, ethical choices, and how the student vetted or modified any AI-suggested code or text.

2. Contextualized & Personalized Prompts 🧩

Method: Hyper-Specific, Unique Context

AI models are trained on general data. To bypass them, make assignments specific to the **class discourse, local environment, or recent, non-public events** discussed only in your lectures. This creates a friction point for generic LLM prompts.

Example: AI Model Application (The Green/Black Test)

“Analyze the **specific dataset we cleaned in Lab 4** and demonstrate how changing **Parameter A by 10%** affects the model's output, using only a visual representation designed with our **class theme colors (white, green, and black)**. Justify your findings by referencing **the slide titled 'Bias Trade-Offs' from Tuesday’s lecture**.”

3. Hands-On & Alternative Modalities 🛠️

Method: Emphasizing "Doing" Over "Writing"

These tasks require the creation of a physical artifact, the documentation of a live execution, or a dynamic presentation, which AIs cannot perform.

Example: Live Cybersecurity Challenge & Defense

  • **Live Forensics:** Require students to perform a practical lab (e.g., a Capture The Flag challenge on a sandbox environment). Submission must include **command-line inputs** and a **screenshot of the successful execution** with a unique lab-generated token.
  • **Oral Defense:** Implement a **"Model Explainability Report"** where students must present a model they trained and orally answer dynamic, *ad-hoc* questions about its structure and why it made a specific erroneous prediction.

4. Metacognitive & Ethical Reflection 🤔

Method: Critique the Tool Itself

Encourage students to use AI but shift the assessment to their ability to critically evaluate and correct the AI's output. This fosters essential digital literacy and ethical awareness.

Example: Ethical Review of AI Output

“Prompt a generative AI tool to draft a Code of Conduct for an AI-powered hiring system. Then, write a **Reflection Essay** that:

  1. Lists the exact prompts used.
  2. Identifies at least two specific **ethical biases** exhibited by the AI's draft.
  3. Justifies every change you made to the text, citing principles of fairness and transparency unique to AI ethics.”
