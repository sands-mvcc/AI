Welcome, Educators!

The goal is not to make assignments "AI-proof" (which is likely impossible), but to design assessments where using AI provides **limited benefit** or where AI use must be **transparent and critically evaluated**. The most effective approach combines multiple strategies.

Use the navigation bar on the left to explore 13 core methods and key best practices for implementation.

1. Personalization and Specificity

Assignments must require students to draw on **unique personal experiences**, specific contexts, or localized information that AI cannot access or replicate authentically.

Example for Cybersecurity: Ask students to assess their own home network security and document specific vulnerabilities found, rather than a generic network.

2. Process-Based Assessment (Scaffolding)

Break large assignments into smaller, interconnected components that track **student thinking and development** over time. AI can generate a final product easily, but not the authentic paper trail.

Example for AI/Programming: Require commit history, code evolution documentation, and explanation of debugging processes for every coding project.

3. Metacognitive and Reflective Components

Require students to **explain their thinking process**, justify their choices, and reflect on their learning journey. AI can produce an answer, but not an authentic reflection on *how* a student arrived at it.

Example: Explain why certain approaches were chosen over alternatives (e.g., why a specific algorithm was selected).

4. Authentic Assessment

Create assignments that mirror **real-world professional tasks** and require application of knowledge in complex, context-rich situations rather than simple recall.

Example for Cybersecurity: Conduct actual penetration testing on authorized systems (like a sandbox environment) with detailed reporting, mimicking a real industry job.

5. Multimodal Assessment

Use non-traditional formats that require the integration of **multiple skills and media types** that AI currently struggles to produce cohesively and uniquely.

Example for Networking: Create video demonstrations of configuration processes with verbal explanations and visual proof of concept.

6. Synchronous and In-Person Components

Incorporate **real-time, face-to-face elements** that cannot be outsourced to AI.

Examples: Oral defenses, live problem-solving demonstrations, in-class writing samples, or "Viva voce" oral examinations.

7. Collaborative and Social Learning Tasks

Assignments requiring **genuine human interaction**, negotiation, and group dynamics. AI can write an essay, but it can't participate in group dynamics.

Examples: Project-based learning with real-world deliverables, or peer review with substantive feedback requirements.

8. Higher-Order Cognitive Tasks (Bloom's Taxonomy)

Design assignments that target **Analysis, Evaluation, Synthesis, and Creation** (upper levels) rather than lower levels (Remember, Understand, Apply).

Example for Security: Evaluate the effectiveness of different security frameworks (e.g., NIST vs. ISO 27001) for a specific organizational context and justify the choice.

9. AI Transparency and Critical Engagement

Explicitly incorporate AI but require students to **document, critique, and improve upon AI outputs**. Turn the AI into a partner or a source to be verified.

Example: Have students input assignment prompts into multiple AI tools, evaluate and compare responses, and then provide a heavily modified, final version with documentation of AI errors/biases.

10. Context-Specific Case Studies

Use detailed, **specific scenarios with multiple variables** that require deep analysis rather than generic responses that AI can easily pull from its training data.

Example for Networking: Troubleshoot specific network diagrams with unique configurations and symptoms that are not publicly documented.

11. Iterative Revision with Instructor Feedback

Assignments built around **multiple drafts with personalized instructor feedback** that students must incorporate and explain.

Key: The personalization of the feedback makes the next revision nearly impossible for a general-purpose AI to complete authentically.

12. Time-Bound Assessment

Assessments completed within **limited timeframes** that make extensive AI consultation impractical or impossible to integrate seamlessly.

Examples: Live coding challenges, in-class essays, or rapid response discussions.

13. Experiential and Field-Based Learning

Assignments grounded in **direct experience outside the classroom** that AI cannot access or fake convincingly.

Example for IT: Site visits to data centers or local businesses with required photographic/video documentation and security assessment reports.

Implementation Best Practices

Clear Communication

  • Establish transparent AI use policies (e.g., "stoplight" model: AI-free/red, AI-assisted/yellow, AI-empowered/green).
  • Develop detailed rubrics that emphasize criteria AI cannot meet well (e.g., originality, personal reflection).

Assessment Literacy

  • Test your own assignments with AI tools to understand their vulnerabilities.
  • Teach students about AI capabilities and limitations as a part of critical literacy.

Focus on Learning Outcomes

  • Ensure assessments measure whether students achieved course objectives.
  • Design assignments students find meaningful and engaging.
