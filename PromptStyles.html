What is Prompt Engineering?

Prompt engineering is the practice of crafting inputs (prompts) to AI systems that yield the most accurate, relevant, and useful outputs. Think of it as learning the "language" that helps AI understand exactly what you need.

🎯 Classroom Analogy

Imagine giving instructions to a substitute teacher who's highly capable but doesn't know your classroom routines. A vague instruction like "teach the students" won't work well. But specific guidance like "You're an experienced 5th-grade science teacher. Use hands-on demonstrations and relate concepts to everyday life" yields much better results. That's prompt engineering.

Why This Matters for Education

  • Student Preparation: Students will use AI throughout their careers; teaching them to use it effectively is essential
  • Enhanced Productivity: Educators can create lesson plans, assessments, and materials more efficiently
  • Critical Thinking: Understanding prompt engineering develops metacognitive skills about communication and problem decomposition
  • Ethical AI Use: Knowing how prompts work helps educators guide students toward responsible AI usage

Style 1 Role-Based Prompting (MIT Style)

📖 Definition

Role-Based prompting assigns a specific persona, profession, or perspective to the AI, leveraging specialized domain knowledge and appropriate communication styles. This technique, popularized at MIT and other leading institutions, helps the AI adopt the right "mindset" for your task.

🎭 Teaching Analogy

Think of an actor preparing for a role. Before performing, they research their character's background, profession, and motivations. Similarly, when you assign a role to AI, you're helping it "get into character" as an expert in that domain. A financial analyst thinks differently than a creative writer, and role-based prompting captures these distinctions.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Persona Assignment: Explicitly state who the AI should "be" (e.g., "You are a high school biology teacher")
  • Expertise Level: Specify the depth of knowledge needed (novice, intermediate, expert)
  • Perspective Framing: Define the viewpoint from which to approach the task
  • Contextual Constraints: Include relevant situational factors (audience, purpose, format)

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Start with "You are...": Begin your prompt by explicitly assigning the role
  2. Add qualifiers: Include expertise level, years of experience, or specific credentials
  3. Define the context: Explain the situation or audience
  4. State the task: Clearly articulate what you need the AI to do

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Lesson Planning

"You are an experienced middle school math teacher with 15 years of classroom experience. Create a 45-minute lesson plan on introducing algebraic expressions to 7th graders who have never seen variables before. Include differentiation strategies for struggling learners."

Example 2: Assessment Creation

"You are a college-level psychology professor specializing in cognitive development. Design a rubric for evaluating student research papers on Piaget's stages of development. The rubric should assess understanding of theory, application of concepts, and critical analysis."

Example 3: Student Support

"You are a patient and encouraging reading specialist working with elementary students. Explain the difference between consonant blends and digraphs using simple language and everyday examples that a 2nd grader would understand."

Style 2 Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting

📖 Definition

Chain-of-Thought prompting instructs the AI to break down complex problems into logical, sequential steps, making the reasoning process transparent and improving accuracy on multi-step tasks. This technique mirrors how expert problem-solvers think aloud.

🧩 Teaching Analogy

Imagine teaching a student to solve a word problem. Instead of jumping to the answer, you guide them: "First, identify what information you have. Next, determine what you need to find. Then, choose the appropriate operation..." CoT prompting teaches AI to follow this same disciplined approach, making errors easier to spot and reasoning clearer.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Step-by-Step Reasoning: Breaking complex problems into manageable sub-tasks
  • Explicit Thinking: Making the reasoning process visible and traceable
  • Logical Sequencing: Following a clear progression from problem to solution
  • Error Detection: Easier to identify where reasoning goes wrong
  • Teaching Tool: Models good problem-solving strategies for students

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Add the instruction: Include phrases like "think step-by-step" or "break this down into steps"
  2. Be specific about steps: If needed, outline the logical sequence you want
  3. Request intermediate work: Ask the AI to show its reasoning at each stage
  4. Verify logic: Check that each step follows from the previous

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Math Problem Solving

"Solve this problem step-by-step: A classroom has 24 students. If 1/3 are absent and 2 more leave early, how many remain? Show your thinking at each step."

Why it works: The AI will calculate 1/3 of 24, subtract that, then subtract 2 more, showing each calculation.

Example 2: Scientific Reasoning

"A student claims plants can grow without sunlight if given extra water. Evaluate this claim step-by-step: (1) Explain what plants need sunlight for, (2) Identify what water provides, (3) Determine if one can replace the other, (4) State your conclusion with evidence."

Example 3: Writing Process

"Help a student develop a thesis statement for an essay on climate change. Think step-by-step: First, identify the specific aspect of climate change they want to focus on. Second, determine their position on that aspect. Third, craft a clear, arguable statement. Fourth, check that it's specific enough for a 5-page essay."

Style 3 Few-Shot Prompting

📖 Definition

Few-Shot prompting provides the AI with 3-5 examples of the desired input-output pattern, tone, or format before presenting the actual task. This "learning by example" approach helps the AI understand exactly what you want without lengthy explanations.

📚 Teaching Analogy

Think about teaching vocabulary to young students. Instead of just defining "mammal," you show examples: "A dog is a mammal. A cat is a mammal. A whale is a mammal. Now, is a eagle a mammal?" Students learn the pattern from examples. Few-Shot prompting works the same way—show the AI several examples, and it infers the rule.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Pattern Recognition: AI learns from examples rather than explicit instructions
  • Consistency: Examples should follow the same format and style
  • Representative Samples: Choose examples that cover the range of inputs you expect
  • 3-5 Sweet Spot: Too few examples miss patterns; too many waste tokens
  • Format Matching: Structure your examples exactly as you want outputs structured

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Select representative examples: Choose 3-5 examples that show the pattern clearly
  2. Use consistent formatting: Keep Input/Output labels and structure identical
  3. Show variety: Include different types of valid inputs/outputs
  4. End with your task: Present your actual question in the same format
  5. Test and refine: If outputs are inconsistent, adjust your examples

Style 4 Zero-Shot Prompting

📖 Definition

Zero-Shot prompting uses direct, clear instructions without providing any examples. It relies on the AI's pre-existing training to understand and complete straightforward tasks. This is the simplest and most efficient prompting style for routine tasks.

🎯 Teaching Analogy

Imagine asking a colleague who teaches English to "please summarize this article in three sentences." You don't need to show examples of summaries—they already know what a summary is and how to create one. Zero-Shot prompting works the same way: the AI already understands common tasks like summarizing, translating, or explaining, so you can simply ask directly.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Direct Instructions: State exactly what you want using clear action verbs
  • No Examples Needed: For common, well-understood tasks
  • Efficiency: Fastest prompt style, uses fewest tokens
  • Action Verbs: Summarize, explain, translate, list, define, compare, analyze
  • Best for Simple Tasks: When the task is unambiguous and commonly performed

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Start with an action verb: Summarize, explain, translate, create, etc.
  2. Specify constraints: Include word count, format, or style requirements
  3. Provide context if needed: Specify audience level or purpose
  4. Be concise: Keep instructions brief and focused

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Content Summarization

"Summarize the attached article about photosynthesis in five bullet points suitable for 8th-grade students."

Example 2: Concept Explanation

"Explain the difference between weather and climate using language appropriate for elementary students."

Example 3: Question Generation

"Create 10 multiple-choice questions based on Chapter 5 of the textbook, covering key vocabulary and main concepts."

Example 4: Translation Task

"Translate this assignment description into Spanish, maintaining a formal academic tone."

Example 5: List Creation

"List 15 real-world applications of quadratic equations that would interest high school students."

  • Explanation: Explain, describe, clarify, demonstrate, illustrate
  • Transformation: Summarize, simplify, translate, convert, rewrite
  • Organization: List, organize, outline, sort, rank, prioritize

Style 5 Instructional Prompting

📖 Definition

Instructional prompting uses specific constraints and delimiters to define precise boundaries around the AI's output, including length limits, format requirements, and structural guidelines. This style ensures outputs meet exact specifications for length, tone, and format.

📐 Teaching Analogy

Think of instructional prompting like giving students a detailed rubric before an assignment. Instead of saying "write an essay," you specify: "Write a 500-700 word argumentative essay with an introduction, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion. Use MLA format. Include at least three cited sources." The constraints create a clear target, making success measurable.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Quantitative Limits: Word count, number of items, time constraints
  • Format Specifications: Structure, style, tone requirements
  • Delimiters: Using symbols (""", ###, ---) to separate sections
  • Boundary Setting: Defining what to include and exclude
  • Professional Standards: Matching output to industry or academic formats

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Define length constraints: Specify word count, sentence limits, or time duration
  2. Set format requirements: Bullet points, paragraphs, tables, etc.
  3. Use delimiters: Separate instructions from content with triple quotes (""") or other markers
  4. Specify tone/style: Formal, conversational, academic, persuasive
  5. List what to exclude: Sometimes stating what NOT to include is helpful

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Executive Summary with Constraints

Write an executive summary of this research report on learning styles. Constraints: - Maximum 150 words - Three paragraphs: Background, Findings, Implications - Professional academic tone - Include no more than three statistics - Use present tense Content to summarize: """ [Research report text would go here] """

Example 2: Lesson Plan Format

Create a lesson plan for teaching the Pythagorean theorem. Requirements: - 60-minute class period - Include: Learning objectives (3), Materials needed, Step-by-step procedure, Assessment method, Differentiation strategies - Format: Use headers for each section - Grade level: 8th grade - Exclude: Homework assignments (not needed) Focus the lesson on practical applications students can relate to.

Example 3: Feedback with Parameters

Provide feedback on this student essay. Guidelines: - Begin with two specific strengths - Identify three areas for improvement - Each point should be 1-2 sentences - Use encouraging, constructive language - End with one concrete next step - Total length: 200-250 words Student essay: """ [Essay text] """

Style 6 Self-Consistency Prompting

📖 Definition

Self-Consistency prompting asks the AI to generate multiple reasoning paths to solve the same problem, then select the most consistent or frequently appearing answer. This technique improves accuracy on complex problems where there might be multiple valid approaches.

🔍 Teaching Analogy

Imagine three math teachers independently solving a complex word problem. If all three arrive at the same answer using different methods, you can be confident it's correct. But if one gets a different answer, you'd investigate further. Self-Consistency prompting applies this "multiple experts" approach—the AI solves the problem several ways and looks for agreement among the solutions.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Multiple Pathways: Generating diverse approaches to the same problem
  • Cross-Verification: Checking if different methods yield same answer
  • Majority Voting: The most common answer is likely correct
  • Error Detection: Inconsistent results flag potential problems
  • Confidence Building: Agreement across methods increases certainty

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Request multiple approaches: Ask for 3-5 different reasoning paths
  2. Vary the methods: Explicitly request different strategies if needed
  3. Compare results: Have AI identify which answers match
  4. Select most consistent: Choose the answer appearing most frequently
  5. Investigate outliers: If answers differ significantly, determine why

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Math Problem Verification

Solve this problem using three different approaches, then identify the most consistent answer: "A teacher has 150 sheets of paper. She gives each of her 24 students the same number of sheets and has 6 left over. How many sheets did each student receive?" Approach 1: Use division and remainder Approach 2: Work backwards from the known remainder Approach 3: Use algebraic equation Then state which answer appears most often and is therefore most reliable.

Example 2: Historical Analysis

Analyze the causes of World War I from three different historical perspectives: 1. Political/diplomatic perspective 2. Economic perspective 3. Social/cultural perspective Identify which causes appear in all three analyses—these are likely the most significant factors.

Example 3: Scientific Reasoning

A plant in a classroom is wilting. Generate three different diagnostic pathways: 1. Consider water-related factors 2. Consider light-related factors 3. Consider soil/nutrient factors For each pathway, determine if it could explain ALL the symptoms. Which explanation is most consistent with all observations?

This prompt style is particularly valuable for teaching students about their own thinking processes. By explicitly showing multiple approaches to the same problem, students learn:

  • There are often multiple valid solution strategies
  • Cross-checking answers increases accuracy
  • Inconsistent results should trigger re-examination
  • Different perspectives can reveal different aspects of a problem

Style 7 Meta-Prompting

📖 Definition

Meta-Prompting uses AI to generate, refine, or improve prompts themselves. Instead of directly asking for content, you ask the AI to help you craft a better prompt for another task. This is "prompting about prompting"—using AI as a prompt engineering assistant.

🎓 Teaching Analogy

Imagine you're designing an assignment but aren't sure how to phrase the instructions clearly. You ask a colleague, "How should I word this assignment so students understand exactly what I want?" They help you craft clearer directions. Meta-prompting does the same—it helps you improve how you ask the AI for help, making your eventual outputs better.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Prompt Optimization: Improving unclear or ineffective prompts
  • Iterative Refinement: Building better prompts through AI assistance
  • Learning Tool: Teaches users what makes prompts effective
  • Template Generation: Creating reusable prompt structures
  • Expertise Transfer: AI shares prompt engineering best practices

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. State your goal: Explain what you ultimately want to accomplish
  2. Request prompt creation: Ask AI to write an effective prompt for that goal
  3. Review and refine: Test the generated prompt, request improvements
  4. Ask for explanation: Have AI explain why the prompt is structured that way
  5. Save successful prompts: Build a library of effective prompt templates

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Creating Assessment Prompts

"I need to assess student understanding of photosynthesis for 7th graders. Write a highly effective prompt I can use to generate a quiz that tests both factual knowledge and conceptual understanding. The quiz should include multiple question types and be appropriate for students reading at grade level."

Example 2: Lesson Planning Meta-Prompt

"Help me create a prompt template I can reuse for generating lesson plans. The template should work for any subject and grade level, include all essential lesson components, and ensure differentiation strategies are included. Show me the template, then explain why each element is included."

Example 3: Improving Vague Requests

"I have this vague prompt: 'Help me teach fractions.' Rewrite this as a clear, detailed prompt that would produce a useful teaching resource. Explain what you added and why."

Example 4: Creating Student-Facing Prompts

"Write a prompt that my high school students can use to get AI help with essay outlining without having AI write the essay for them. The prompt should guide students through their own thinking process while preventing academic dishonesty."

Style 8 ReAct (Reason + Act)

📖 Definition

ReAct prompting combines reasoning with action execution, instructing the AI to first think through a problem logically and then perform specific actions like searching for information, running calculations, or accessing tools. This creates a cycle of reasoning and acting that mirrors how humans solve complex, real-world problems.

🔬 Teaching Analogy

Imagine a scientist conducting an experiment. They don't just jump to action—they first reason: "I need to measure pH levels to test my hypothesis." Then they act: they get a pH meter and conduct the test. Then they reason again: "The pH is 3.5, which means..." This cycle of think-then-do-then-think is exactly what ReAct prompting accomplishes.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Reasoning Phase: AI explains what it needs to do and why
  • Action Phase: AI performs specific tasks (search, calculate, retrieve data)
  • Iterative Process: Multiple reason→act cycles may be needed
  • Tool Integration: Combines AI reasoning with external resources
  • Dynamic Problem-Solving: Adapts based on information discovered

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Request reasoning first: Ask AI to explain what information it needs
  2. Specify available actions: Tell AI what tools or resources it can use
  3. Structure the cycle: "First reason what's needed, then [action], then reason about results"
  4. Allow iteration: Let AI repeat the cycle if initial information is insufficient
  5. Request synthesis: After actions, ask AI to integrate findings into final answer

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Research-Based Lesson Planning

Create a lesson plan on renewable energy for 6th graders using the ReAct approach: 1. REASON: What information do you need about current renewable energy usage to make this relevant? 2. ACT: Search for recent statistics on renewable energy adoption in the US 3. REASON: Based on those findings, what teaching approach would be most engaging? 4. ACT: Create the lesson plan incorporating the real-world data Show your reasoning at each step before taking action.

Example 2: Current Events Integration

Help me create discussion questions about economic policy for my high school government class: 1. REASON: What recent economic policy changes should students be aware of? 2. ACT: Look up current Federal Reserve interest rate decisions 3. REASON: How does this connect to our curriculum on monetary policy? 4. ACT: Generate 5 discussion questions linking current events to course concepts Explain your reasoning before each action.

Example 3: Fact-Checking Student Claims

A student claims "Most scientists don't believe in climate change." Use ReAct to verify: 1. REASON: What evidence would confirm or refute this claim? 2. ACT: Search for scientific consensus data on climate change 3. REASON: Is the student's claim accurate based on this evidence? 4. ACT: Draft a response correcting the misconception with evidence Show each reasoning step before searching or responding.

Style 9 Generated Knowledge Prompting

📖 Definition

Generated Knowledge prompting instructs the AI to first list or generate relevant background facts about a topic before answering a complex question. This technique improves answer quality by ensuring the AI has activated relevant knowledge before reasoning about the problem.

📚 Teaching Analogy

Before writing an essay, good students brainstorm what they know about the topic. They list key facts, important dates, relevant concepts—activating prior knowledge before attempting to construct an argument. Generated Knowledge prompting makes AI do the same: recall relevant information first, then use that foundation to build the answer.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Knowledge Activation: Bringing relevant facts to the forefront before reasoning
  • Two-Step Process: Generate knowledge, then apply it to the question
  • Improved Accuracy: Having facts "on the table" reduces errors
  • Transparency: Shows what information is being used to reach conclusions
  • Teaching Tool: Models how to activate prior knowledge before problem-solving

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Request knowledge generation first: "First, list 3-5 key facts about..."
  2. Be specific about relevance: Indicate what type of facts are needed
  3. Then pose the question: "Based on those facts, explain/analyze..."
  4. Request explicit connection: Ask AI to show how facts support the answer
  5. Verify fact accuracy: Check that generated facts are correct

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Science Concept Explanation

First, list three key facts about how neurons transmit signals. Based on those facts, explain why certain drugs can affect mood and behavior. Make explicit connections between the facts you listed and your explanation.

Example 2: Historical Analysis

Before analyzing the causes of the Civil War, first list: - 3 economic differences between North and South - 2 key political disagreements - 2 social/cultural factors Then, using these facts, explain which factor was most significant and why. Reference specific facts in your analysis.

Example 3: Literature Interpretation

First, identify three key symbols used in "The Great Gatsby" and what each represents. Based on these symbols, analyze what Fitzgerald is saying about the American Dream. Show how each symbol supports your interpretation.

Example 4: Math Problem Context

Before solving this problem, list three important properties of right triangles. Problem: A 25-foot ladder leans against a wall, reaching 20 feet high. How far is the base of the ladder from the wall? Use the properties you listed to solve this problem step-by-step.

💡 Why This Works: The Psychology

Generated Knowledge prompting leverages several cognitive principles:

  • Priming: Activating relevant concepts makes them more accessible for reasoning
  • Working Memory Management: Having facts explicitly stated reduces cognitive load
  • Schema Activation: Listing facts activates related knowledge networks
  • Explicit Reasoning: Makes the connection between facts and conclusions visible

This same technique improves human learning and problem-solving, making it valuable to teach students this approach for their own work.

Style 10 Style Matching

📖 Definition

Style Matching prompting provides the AI with a reference text sample and asks it to replicate that specific writing style, tone, vocabulary level, or rhetorical approach. This technique is powerful for maintaining consistency across documents or matching content to specific audiences or formats.

🎨 Teaching Analogy

Think of an art student studying the masters. To learn Impressionism, they examine Monet's brushstrokes, color palette, and light treatment, then create their own painting "in the style of Monet." Style Matching prompting works similarly—you show AI an example of the style you want, and it creates new content matching those characteristics.

🔑 Key Concepts

  • Reference Text: Providing an exemplar for AI to analyze and emulate
  • Style Elements: Tone, vocabulary, sentence structure, rhetorical devices
  • Consistency: Maintaining uniform voice across multiple pieces
  • Audience Matching: Adapting content to specific reading levels or demographics
  • Brand Voice: Maintaining consistent institutional or personal writing style

📋 How to Use It (Directions)

  1. Select representative sample: Choose text that clearly demonstrates the desired style
  2. Provide the sample: Include the reference text in your prompt
  3. Be explicit about matching: Use phrases like "write in the style of this example"
  4. Specify what to match: Tone? Vocabulary? Sentence length? Structure?
  5. Present your content request: State what new content you need in that style

💡 Educational Examples

Example 1: Matching Reading Level

Here's a passage written for 5th graders: "The water cycle is nature's way of recycling water. First, the sun heats water in lakes and oceans, turning it into invisible water vapor. This process is called evaporation. Next, the water vapor rises into the sky and cools down, forming tiny droplets that cluster together as clouds." Write a similar explanation of the rock cycle in the same style—simple vocabulary, short sentences, clear sequence words like "first" and "next," and everyday comparisons.

Example 2: Matching Instructional Tone

Match the encouraging, supportive tone of this feedback: "You're making great progress with multiplication! I noticed you correctly solved 7 of the 10 problems. Let's look at problem #3 together—what strategy did you use there? Sometimes drawing groups can help us visualize what's happening. Want to try that approach on the next few?" Provide similar feedback for a student struggling with subject-verb agreement.

Example 3: Matching Academic Writing Style

Here's a sample paragraph from a college-level research paper: "Recent empirical evidence suggests that inquiry-based learning significantly impacts student engagement (Martinez, 2023). However, implementation challenges persist, particularly regarding adequate teacher preparation and resource allocation. This study examines three urban schools that successfully adopted inquiry-based pedagogy despite these constraints." Write an introduction paragraph about project-based learning using this same formal, research-oriented style with proper citations and hedging language.

Example 4: Matching Newsletter Voice

Our school newsletter uses this friendly, conversational style: "What an exciting week in Room 12! Our third graders dove into a hands-on geometry project, building 3D shapes from toothpicks and marshmallows. (Pro tip: Keep extra marshmallows handy—they have a tendency to disappear!) Ask your child to explain the difference between a cube and a rectangular prism. You might be surprised by what they know!" Write a similar newsletter update about our upcoming science fair using the same enthusiastic, conversational tone with the occasional aside to parents.
