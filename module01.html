AI in Teaching and Learning

Understanding how artificial intelligence is reshaping the educator's role, the learner's experience, and the institution's responsibility.

Why This Matters

AI is no longer a future consideration for education — it is a present reality. Students arrive in your classroom already using AI tools. Your role is not to gatekeep these tools, but to model critical, ethical, and effective use.

The Shift AI Is Driving in Education

Artificial intelligence is fundamentally changing three dimensions of the educational experience: how content is delivered, how learning is assessed, and how institutions allocate resources. These shifts are not incremental improvements — they represent a structural change to the relationship between instructor, student, and curriculum.

For decades, the dominant model of instruction was transmission: an expert instructor transfers knowledge to a class of learners through lecture, demonstration, and assessment. AI disrupts this model by enabling personalization at scale — a single instructor can now provide differentiated learning experiences, instant feedback, and adaptive content to hundreds of students simultaneously.

🎯

Personalized Learning

AI adapts content pace, difficulty, and format to individual learner needs — what took a tutor to achieve is now accessible to every student at any time.

⚡

Instant Feedback Loops

AI tools provide immediate, specific feedback on drafts, code, math work, and essays — compressing the learning cycle from days to seconds.

♿

Accessibility at Scale

Real-time captioning, translation, text-to-speech, and reading-level adjustment make content accessible to diverse learners without additional instructor workload.

📊

Data-Informed Instruction

Learning analytics surfaced by AI help instructors identify struggling students early, spot common misconceptions, and adjust instruction before it's too late.

The Educator's Evolving Role

A common fear is that AI will replace teachers. The evidence strongly suggests the opposite: AI amplifies the uniquely human aspects of teaching — mentorship, critical thinking facilitation, emotional support, and contextual judgment — while automating the repetitive and administrative tasks that consume educator time.

AI in the Learner Experience

Students are not passive recipients of AI's influence in education — they are active users of AI tools outside the classroom, whether instructors acknowledge it or not. Research from 2024 indicates that over 87% of college students have used generative AI for academic work. The question is not whether students will use AI, but whether they will use it skillfully and ethically.

⚠ The Academic Integrity Paradox

Banning AI tools from the classroom does not prevent students from using them — it simply prevents them from learning to use them well. The educator's task is to design assessments and learning experiences where AI use is scaffolded, transparent, and educationally purposeful.

Institutional Responsibilities

Educational institutions face AI-driven decisions at the policy, infrastructure, and workforce levels. Community colleges, in particular, serve populations with the highest exposure to AI-driven labor market disruption — making AI literacy a workforce equity issue, not just a pedagogical preference.

AI Fundamentals

A rigorous but accessible introduction to how AI systems actually work — from the Turing Test to transformer architectures.

What Is Artificial Intelligence?

Artificial intelligence is the field of computer science dedicated to building systems that perform tasks which, when done by humans, would require intelligence. The term was coined in 1956 by John McCarthy, who defined AI as "the science and engineering of making intelligent machines."

Modern AI is more precisely defined as: computational systems that learn from data, identify patterns, and make decisions or predictions with varying degrees of autonomy. The key word is learn — most contemporary AI systems are not explicitly programmed with rules, but rather trained on large datasets to discover those rules themselves.

The Turing Test (1950)

Alan Turing proposed that a machine could be considered intelligent if it could engage in conversation indistinguishable from a human. Modern large language models like ChatGPT and Claude pass most versions of this test — though researchers argue the test measures conversational fluency, not genuine understanding.

The AI Hierarchy: AI → ML → DL

These three terms are frequently used interchangeably in popular media, but they describe a precise hierarchy of concepts:

🤖

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

The broadest category — any technique enabling machines to mimic human intelligence. Includes rule-based systems, expert systems, and machine learning.

📈

Machine Learning (ML)

A subset of AI in which systems learn from data without explicit programming. Algorithms improve their performance on a task through experience (training data).

🕸️

Deep Learning (DL)

A subset of ML using multi-layered artificial neural networks. Excels at unstructured data (images, audio, text). Powers modern generative AI.

How Machine Learning Works

Traditional programming follows explicit rules: if temperature > 100°C then boil. Machine learning inverts this: given thousands of examples of boiling and not boiling, the algorithm discovers the rule. There are three primary learning paradigms:

ParadigmHow It LearnsClassroom AnalogyExample Application
Supervised LearningLabeled input-output pairsGraded homework with answer keyEmail spam detection, image classification
Unsupervised LearningUnlabeled data — finds patternsStudents grouping concepts without guidanceCustomer segmentation, anomaly detection
Reinforcement LearningRewards and penalties for actionsVideo game score optimizationGame AI, robotics, recommendation systems

Neural Networks and Deep Learning

Neural networks are loosely inspired by the structure of the human brain. They consist of layers of nodes (neurons) connected by weighted edges. During training, the network adjusts these weights to minimize prediction error — a process called backpropagation.

Deep learning refers to neural networks with many hidden layers (hence "deep"). These architectures excel at processing unstructured data — images, audio, and text — and are the engine behind every major AI product you use today: ChatGPT, image generators, speech recognition, and recommendation algorithms.

✅ Generative AI Defined

Generative AI is a class of deep learning models trained to produce new content — text, images, audio, video, or code — by learning the statistical patterns of their training data. Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4o, Claude, and Gemini are generative AI systems trained on vast text corpora.

Narrow AI vs. General AI vs. AGI

🎯

Narrow AI (ANI)

AI designed to perform a specific task. All commercial AI today is narrow AI — excellent at one domain, useless outside it. ChatGPT is narrow AI for conversation.

🌐

Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)

A hypothetical AI with human-level general reasoning across all domains. Does not yet exist. Most researchers estimate AGI is decades away — if achievable at all.

AI Vocabulary

The essential lexicon every educator needs to participate confidently in conversations about AI — from tokens to training data.

Why Vocabulary Matters

Precise language shapes clear thinking. When educators use AI terms correctly, they model intellectual rigor for students, engage more effectively with technical colleagues, and navigate institutional AI policy discussions with authority.

Foundational Terms

Algorithm

A step-by-step set of instructions for solving a problem. AI algorithms learn optimal instructions from data rather than being explicitly programmed.

Training Data

The dataset used to teach a machine learning model. Model quality, bias, and capability are directly shaped by what training data is included — and excluded.

Model

A mathematical representation of patterns learned from training data. When you "use ChatGPT," you are querying a model — specifically a large language model.

Parameter

A numerical weight in a neural network adjusted during training. GPT-4 has an estimated 1 trillion parameters. More parameters generally mean greater capability and cost.

Token

The unit of text an LLM processes — roughly ¾ of a word. "Education" = 1 token. Token limits constrain how much text a model can process in one interaction.

Prompt

The input text given to an AI model. The quality of the prompt directly determines the quality of the output — a core skill covered in Module 3.

Inference

The process of using a trained model to generate outputs. When you submit a prompt, the model performs inference — applying learned patterns to your specific input.

Fine-Tuning

Further training a pre-trained model on a specific dataset to improve performance on a targeted task — like fine-tuning GPT on medical literature for clinical Q&A.

Generative AI Terms

Large Language Model (LLM)

A deep learning model trained on massive text corpora to understand and generate human-like text. GPT-4o, Claude 3, and Gemini are LLMs.

Hallucination

When an AI model generates confident, plausible-sounding content that is factually incorrect. A critical limitation educators must teach students to recognize and verify.

Context Window

The maximum amount of text (tokens) a model can consider at once. Models "forget" content outside their context window — like working memory, not long-term memory.

Temperature

A parameter controlling output randomness. Low temperature (0.1) = predictable, focused outputs. High temperature (1.0) = creative, varied outputs. Affects reliability.

Multimodal AI

AI that processes and generates multiple input/output types — text, images, audio, and video. GPT-4o and Gemini Ultra are multimodal.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

An architecture that combines an LLM with a live knowledge retrieval system, enabling the model to access current information beyond its training cutoff.

Ethics and Policy Terms

Algorithmic Bias

Systematic errors in AI outputs that unfairly disadvantage particular groups — typically inherited from biased training data or design choices.

Explainability (XAI)

The degree to which an AI system's decisions can be understood and interpreted by humans. Critical for high-stakes applications like hiring, lending, and healthcare.

Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)

A formal institutional or classroom policy defining permitted and prohibited uses of AI tools — a deliverable you will create in Module 10 of this workshop.

Generative AI Watermarking

Technical methods — including C2PA metadata standards — for embedding signals in AI-generated content to identify its origin. Increasingly important for academic integrity.

AI Myths & Misconceptions

Separating what AI actually does from the hype, fear, and misunderstanding that dominate public discourse — and your students' questions.

Why This Matters for Educators

Your students arrive with beliefs about AI shaped by science fiction, news media, and social media — most of which are inaccurate. Correcting foundational misconceptions early is essential for developing students who can reason critically about AI rather than react emotionally to it.

Common Myths — Click to Reveal the Reality

❌

Myth 1: AI understands what it's saying

+ Reveal

AI language models predict statistically likely next tokens based on training data patterns. There is no comprehension, intent, or awareness behind the output — only sophisticated pattern matching at massive scale.

Reality

LLMs are "stochastic parrots" — extraordinarily good at pattern-based text generation, but they do not understand, believe, or intend anything they produce. This is why they hallucinate confidently and why human verification remains essential.

❌

Myth 2: AI will replace teachers

+ Reveal

This fear is understandable but not supported by evidence. Teaching involves mentorship, relationship, contextual judgment, ethical modeling, and emotional intelligence — none of which AI can replicate.

Reality

AI automates routine and scalable tasks (drafting, grading objective items, generating practice problems) while freeing educators to focus on the high-value human dimensions of teaching. The risk is not replacement — it's that educators who refuse to engage with AI will be outpaced by those who do.

❌

Myth 3: AI is always accurate

+ Reveal

AI models hallucinate, produce outdated information, reflect training data biases, and present incorrect information with the same confident tone as correct information.

Reality

AI output requires human verification — especially for facts, citations, statistics, and technical claims. Teaching students to verify AI outputs is a critical information literacy skill for the 21st century.

❌

Myth 4: Using AI is cheating

+ Reveal

This framing is outdated. The same logic once applied to calculators, spell-checkers, and the internet. Tool use is not inherently dishonest — undisclosed tool use may be.

Reality

The academic integrity issue is undisclosed AI use, not AI use itself. Well-designed AI policies distinguish between AI-as-scaffold (acceptable) and AI-as-substitute-for-learning (problematic). Module 10 covers AUP design in depth.

❌

Myth 5: AI is too complex for non-technical educators

+ Reveal

You do not need to understand how to build AI to use it effectively — just as you don't need to understand combustion to drive a car. Effective AI use requires pedagogical judgment, not computer science credentials.

Reality

The skills that make great educators — clear communication, subject expertise, knowledge of learner needs, and ethical judgment — are exactly the skills that make AI tools more powerful. This workshop exists to close the confidence gap, not the expertise gap.

❌

Myth 6: AI is objective and unbiased

+ Reveal

AI systems inherit the biases of their training data, which reflects historical human decisions, power structures, and cultural assumptions. They can amplify these biases at scale.

Reality

AI outputs must be critically evaluated for bias — particularly in high-stakes applications like career advice, hiring suggestions, or criminal justice. Bias awareness is a core AI literacy competency covered in Module 10.

AI Occupation Impact

How AI is reshaping the labor market — which occupations are most exposed, which are resilient, and what this means for the students in your classroom today.

The Labor Market Transformation

AI is driving the most significant restructuring of the labor market since the Industrial Revolution. The key distinction from previous automation waves is that AI targets cognitive work — not just manual or routine tasks. This means white-collar, knowledge-economy occupations face disruption at unprecedented scale.

A 2024 McKinsey Global Institute analysis estimates that between 40–60% of worker activities in developed economies could be automated or significantly augmented by AI by 2030. This does not mean job elimination — most occupations will be transformed rather than eliminated, with new AI-assisted workflows, roles, and required competencies.

What AI Replaces vs. What It Amplifies

⚠ Tasks AI Will Replace or Reduce

  • Routine data entry and form processing
  • Basic report generation from structured data
  • First-draft document creation
  • Scheduling and calendar management
  • Basic customer service interactions
  • Repetitive code generation

✅ Skills AI Will Amplify

  • Critical evaluation of AI-generated outputs
  • Creative problem framing and design
  • Interpersonal communication and empathy
  • Ethical decision-making under uncertainty
  • Cross-domain synthesis and innovation
  • Teaching, mentoring, and human development

Implications for Your Classroom

The students in your classroom today will spend 30–40 years in a workforce fundamentally different from the one that shaped your curriculum. The most important thing you can teach them is not just what to know, but how to work alongside AI — critically, ethically, and effectively.

📌 Curriculum Implication

Bloom's Taxonomy needs reframing for the AI era. If AI can produce Remember and Understand-level outputs on demand, then educationally valuable work must operate at Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create levels — where human judgment and context cannot be replicated by pattern matching.

For Your Students

AI careers span every sector and educational level — from 2-year technical certifications to advanced research degrees. Community college students are uniquely positioned to enter AI-adjacent roles in cybersecurity, data analysis, healthcare informatics, and technology operations.

AI in 2026

The current state of the AI landscape — what has changed, what capabilities are emerging, and what educators need to know right now.

📌 Current State — May 2026

AI capabilities have advanced significantly in the past 24 months. Multimodal models now handle text, images, audio, video, and code simultaneously. Agentic AI systems can plan and execute multi-step tasks autonomously. AI is embedded in nearly every productivity tool your students and colleagues use daily.

Defining Trends in 2026

🤝

Agentic AI

AI systems that take sequences of actions autonomously — browsing the web, writing and executing code, managing files, and interacting with external services. Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini all offer agentic capabilities in 2026.

🎭

Multimodal Reasoning

Current frontier models process and reason across text, images, audio, video, and documents simultaneously. A student can now photograph a textbook page and have an AI explain, question, and quiz them on it in real time.

🏃

AI on Device

Smaller, efficient AI models now run locally on laptops and phones without cloud connectivity. This shifts privacy concerns and enables offline educational applications — including in under-resourced settings.

⚖️

Regulatory Acceleration

The EU AI Act is fully in force. The US has issued executive guidance. Institutions face compliance obligations around AI transparency, bias auditing, and data governance — directly affecting how AI can be used in education.

🛡️

AI-Generated Content Detection

Detection tools have become more sophisticated but remain imperfect. C2PA content provenance standards are being adopted by major platforms. The arms race between generation and detection continues to shape academic integrity policy.

📚

AI in Every Learning Tool

In 2026, AI is embedded in Google Classroom, Canvas, Blackboard, Microsoft Teams for Education, and virtually every productivity tool. "Using AI" is no longer optional — it's baked into the infrastructure.

The Capability Frontier: What's New

🧠

Reasoning Models

Models like o3 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet use "chain of thought" reasoning to solve complex multi-step problems — including advanced math, scientific analysis, and legal reasoning — with significantly higher accuracy than earlier models.

🎬

Video Generation

High-quality AI video generation (Sora, Runway Gen-3, Kling) can produce educational explainer videos from a text prompt. The cost of producing instructional media has dropped dramatically.

🔊

Realistic Voice Synthesis

ElevenLabs and similar tools produce indistinguishable synthetic voices from minutes of sample audio. NotebookLM generates podcast-style audio summaries. Audio course materials are now trivially producible.

🤖

Custom AI Agents

Educators can now deploy custom AI tutors with specific knowledge, personas, and constraints — without coding. These tools are directly applicable to office-hours support, study assistance, and lab guidance.

What This Means for Your Course Design Today

🎯 Action Items for Educators in 2026

  • Audit your existing assessments: Any assignment that can be completed by current AI tools with a single prompt needs redesign toward higher-order thinking, process documentation, or in-class demonstration.
  • Update your syllabus AI policy: Students deserve clear guidance on what AI use is encouraged, permitted, or prohibited — not ambiguity that disadvantages first-generation students.
  • Explore one new AI tool per module: This workshop gives you hands-on experience with the tools your students are already using. The best preparation is direct engagement.
  • Connect AI to your discipline: Every field has AI applications your students will encounter in their careers. Module 6 helps you build discipline-specific AI-enhanced materials.
